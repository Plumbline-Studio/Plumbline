A commercial fabricator

A lead-time clock that waits for the customer

A fabricator's due dates started the day a deposit cleared, before anyone knew what was ordered or where it shipped. Now customers confirm their order through a private link, and the clock starts then.

What changed

Before

The fabricator's tracker gave every job a due date the moment the deposit cleared: deposit date plus the quoted lead time. At that point nobody yet knew where the order shipped, who would sign for it, or whether drawings needed approval. The shop planned against a date that was going to slip, and no one could point at why. Customers confirmed their details on a PDF form.

After

Each customer gets a private link to their own order. They see where it stands, open their estimate, and confirm the details the PDF used to collect. The lead time starts the day they confirm. Until then the due date reads "Set on confirmation", on the customer's screen and in the shop's tracker alike, so a blank date means one thing everyone understands: waiting on the customer.

What the client got

  • A due date that means something, because it starts when the order is actually ready to build.
  • No new system for the team. They keep working in the tracker they already use; the portal reads from it and writes back to it.
  • One link per order, no passwords, and no customer ever sees another customer's order.
  • Reminders that go out on their own, and every customer change logged where the team can see it.
  • The switch made safely: every job's existing due date stayed exactly where it was.

What I built

  • The re-anchor. The tracker's Due formula moved from the deposit date to a new Confirmation Complete anchor. 171 cells were rewritten on the live tracker by script, after a backup, and checked against it: 39 of 39 open jobs kept their due date to the day. A dry-run tool now catches any new row that arrives on the old formula.
  • A portal with no database of its own. A static page plus Vercel serverless functions in Node. The client's Google Sheet, behind their AppSheet app, stays the system of record. The portal reads it and writes single cells back through the Sheets API.
  • Private links without accounts. Each order link carries an HMAC token. A wrong token gets a bare 404, so a probe can't tell "no such order" from "wrong link".
  • An allow-list, not a copy. The order response is built field by field from an allow-list, so a new internal column on the tracker can never reach a customer by default.
  • Documents without exposing Drive. Estimates and drawings stream through the server after the token and ownership checks, so the Drive link never reaches the browser.
  • Mail and reminders. SendGrid from the client's own domain, a daily cron for invites, confirmation nudges and shipping reminders, and a review mode that sends every message to the client's reviewers instead of customers, with a pass-through so named customers can receive their own order's mail.
  • Estimate auto-fill. A tool that reads the estimate and fills only empty tracker cells, never overwriting what a person typed, and flags disagreements instead of picking a winner.
  • Safety rails. A health endpoint that turns red on missing settings, tabs or columns, an audit log of every portal change, and tests that keep customer documents out of the public site.

Connects to

The client's team made the app-side changes in their own AppSheet app and granted read-only access to their Drive. Rollout is careful by design: the portal went live against the real tracker in a review period, with mail going to the client's own reviewers before customers. Since November 3, named customers have received their own order's mail directly. The owner has walked the whole flow and signed off. The shop is keeping the review period on a little longer by its own choice.

The blueprint

Partway through the build, the client and I agreed in writing what "done" would mean: a short list of conditions, each one either true or not. This is that list as of November 16, 2026: every condition is met. Agreed October 14, 2026.

  1. Met · Nov 3, 2026The shop sends a portal invite from its live tracker, and the customer receives it from the shop's own email domain.The first customer received their own order's email from the shop's domain on November 3, and a second on November 9.
  2. Met · Oct 2, 2026A customer opens their link and sees their real job: status, dates and documents, with no dead buttons.
  3. Met · Oct 2, 2026A customer confirms the order, the tracker updates, and the lead-time clock starts.
  4. Met · Nov 16, 2026Every customer email looks right the first time it sends.The client's wording changes are in, and every email, including the ones those two customers received, is correct.
  5. Met · Sept 11, 2026Estimates and job documents reach the portal through the agreed file-sharing route.Read-only access to one folder; the portal only serves a file named on that order's own row.
  6. Met · Oct 2, 2026The shop updates a job, and the portal shows the change.
  7. Met · Sept 8, 2026Emails come from the shop's own address, never from a test account.The first one landed in the inbox, not spam.
  8. MetThe owner walks the whole flow, start to finish, and signs off.He walked it himself and signed off. The exact date isn't recorded.

Ruled out before the build:

  • Any accounting-system integration.
  • Customer uploads filing themselves into the job.
  • Redesigning the tracker's existing columns.
  • Merging the email suite into fewer emails (quoted separately).
  • Redesigning the status steps.
